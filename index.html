After the Peak

U.S. overdose deaths fell about 41% from their mid-2023 peak - the sharpest decline in the data. But the drop is uneven: some states are down two-thirds from their peak, while New Mexico has not peaked yet. This page traces where deaths fell, how fast, by which drug, and who got left out.

Datapulse · Derek Pham and Mustafa Hayeri · Data: CDC NCHS, VSRR Provisional Drug Overdose Death Counts rough prototype

01 / The national turn

The country passed its peak in mid-2023

Each point is the number of overdose deaths in the 12 months ending that month, so seasonal noise is already smoothed out. The total climbed for years, peaked at about 111,000 in the year ending June 2023, then fell over the next 35 months to about 65,000 - roughly where it stood in early 2020.

02 / Which drugs came down

Fentanyl drove the drop; heroin was already gone

Each drug is drawn as a percent of its own peak (peak = 100), so drugs of very different sizes share one scale. Synthetic opioids (fentanyl) are down about 56% from their 2023 peak; heroin is down about 90% since 2017. A single death can involve more than one drug and is counted under each, so the lines cannot be added together.

03 / Follow your own state

The decline did not reach everywhere

The map shades each state by how far its 12-month total has fallen from its own peak. 31 of the 50 states and D.C. fell further than the country as a whole; New Mexico (outlined) is the one state still at its peak. Click a state - or pick one below - to trace its death rate per 100,000 against the national rate.

04 / How far each state fell rough draft

Death rates at the peak vs. now

Each line connects a state's death rate per 100,000 at its own peak to its rate now. Most slope down steeply; a few stay flat or tick up. West Virginia had the highest peak rate in the country (87 per 100k) and has roughly halved it; New Mexico is the lone line still at its top.

05 / The biggest drops, and the holdouts rough draft

Five states that fell the most, five that barely moved

Each spark is one state's death rate over time, with its peak marked. The top row is the five largest declines from peak; the bottom row is the five states closest to their peak today.

06 / The peaks were years apart rough draft

Every state peaked on its own clock

One dot per state, placed at the month it peaked and its peak rate per 100,000. The peaks spread from 2017 to 2026, so a single "post-peak" story hides very different timelines.

Prototype write-up

What we have built so far

The data pipeline is done and merged: Mustafa extended the Project 3 build script so that for every state and drug we now carry the peak month, the peak value, the latest value, the percent change from peak, and the death rate per 100,000 at both points, all recomputed straight from the CDC file. On top of that data, this page lays out the full "After the Peak" story as six rough charts in reading order - the national turn, the decline split by drug, an uneven map, a peak-vs-now slope chart, small multiples of the biggest drops and the holdouts, and a peak-timing plot. The one interaction that works today is the pick-your-state view: clicking a state on the map, or choosing it from the dropdown, redraws its death rate against the national line and reads out how far it has come down from its own peak. Every number on the page is pulled live from data.json, so the charts stay correct when the CDC data is refreshed, and the original Project 3 explorer still lives at explorer.html for reference.

What will be hardest to design, and why

The hardest part will be the scroll-driven transitions: we want the national line, the drug lines, and the map to be one chart that morphs as the reader scrolls, which means binding every mark to a stable key so D3 reuses it across steps instead of redrawing, rather than six charts stacked down the page. Comparing states fairly is the second problem, because they peaked years apart and at very different rates, so we have to decide when to index to each state's own peak and when to show raw deaths per 100,000, and the pick-your-state view has to make that choice legible. Third, Derek's three charts do not yet scale: a slope chart and a small-multiples grid for 51 states get crowded fast, so the real work is deciding what to label, what to fade, and what to drop without losing the outliers that carry the story. Finally, the small states (D.C., Rhode Island, Delaware) nearly disappear on a geographic map, so we may move to Mustafa's tile-grid version, and we still have to write tight per-step annotations that explain each turn without burying the charts in text.

Source: CDC NCHS, VSRR Provisional Drug Overdose Death Counts (12-month-ending counts, provisional, through May 2026). New York includes New York City; rates use each state's 2020 Census population. Counts are the reported provisional values, not the CDC's modeled predicted counts, so the most recent months may revise upward.
